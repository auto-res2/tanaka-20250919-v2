    Title:Beyond Imitation: Leveraging Fine-grained Quality Signals for Alignment
Main Contributions:This paper addresses the limitations of existing LLM alignment methods, specifically the intricacy of RLHF and the superficial imitation learning of SFT due to response-level quality signals. The main contributions include proposing FIGA, a novel alignment approach that leverages fine-grained (token or phrase level) quality signals. This involves curating a refined alignment dataset (SPA) by pairing initial subpar LLM responses with corresponding minimally revised high-quality responses from a powerful LLM (e.g., ChatGPT) to reduce distribution shifts and focus on model weaknesses. Furthermore, a new loss function is devised that utilizes Levenshtein distance to assign specific token-level weights, effectively encouraging the learning of desired actions and penalizing undesired actions. Extensive experiments demonstrate FIGA's superior performance, outperforming initial supervised-finetuned models by 3.2 points and strong PPO methods by 1.8 points, indicating improved alignment with human preferences and enhanced overall task-solving capabilities.
Methodology:The proposed approach, FIGA, is an alignment method similar to SFT but integrates fine-grained quality signals without reinforcement learning. It involves two main steps: 1) **Curated Alignment Dataset (SPA) Construction**: An initial model (Alpaca-7b) generates responses (ˆY) from a diverse pool of queries. A reward model (OpenAssistant/reward-model-deberta-v3-large-v2) filters these instances, retaining only those where the initial response is subpar (RˆY < η1), the ground-truth response is high-quality (RY > η2), and there's a clear quality difference (RY − RˆY > η3). A powerful LLM (gpt-3.5-turbo) then minimally revises the subpar initial responses (ˆY) based on ground-truth (Y), creating high-quality revised responses (˜Y) while maintaining the original linguistic style and reducing distribution shifts. This revision process targets specific low-quality reasons (e.g., inaccuracy, lack of detail). 2) **Fine-grained Quality-Aware Alignment Tuning**: Levenshtein distance is used to compare ˆY and ˜Y, identifying tokens that were newly added, deleted, or substituted. Two token-level weighting functions, ˜r(·) and ˆr(·), are applied: ˜r(·) assigns a positive weight (α=1) to desired tokens (added or substituted in ˜Y), and ˆr(·) assigns a positive weight (β=0.5) to undesired tokens (deleted or substituted from ˆY). Tokens that remain unchanged are assigned a zero weight (γ=0). The learning objective is a modified cross-entropy loss that increases the log-probability of desired tokens from ˜Y and decreases the log-probability of undesired tokens from ˆY, effectively guiding the LLM to understand and adopt preferred behaviors.
Experimental Setup:The base model for generating initial responses was Alpaca-7b. The SPA dataset was constructed using an instance pool comprising HH-RLHF, ShareGPT, Instruct GPT-J Pairwise, SHP, and OpenOrca. The reward model used for filtering instances in SPA dataset construction was OpenAssistant/reward-model-deberta-v3-large-v2, with empirical thresholds set at η1 = 1, η2 = 3, and η3 = 3.5. The revision of initial responses to create the SPA dataset was performed by gpt-3.5-turbo. The final SPA dataset consisted of 17,333 instances. FIGA's hyper-parameters were set as α = 1, β = 0.5, and γ = 0, with an additional filter retaining bad tokens with a negative log-likelihood less than 0.6. Baselines for comparison included SFT, PPO, CoH, RRHF, and DPO. PPO was also evaluated with an expanded dataset of 84,908 entries (PPO (85K)). Evaluation was conducted on a test set of 3,608 data entries using reward scores, and on comprehensive out-of-distribution benchmarks: MMLU (knowledge utilization), TruthfulQA, CrowS-Pairs, and WinoGender (human alignment), and Vicuna and WizardLM (open-ended generation, rated by ChatGPT). Human evaluation was also performed to assess error types and overall quality through pairwise comparisons.
Limitations:One implicit limitation is the reliance on a reward model for initial dataset filtering, which, as acknowledged in the paper, can suffer from "intrinsic inaccuracies of the reward scores, especially in out-of-distribution scenarios," potentially impacting the quality and representativeness of the filtered training data. Additionally, the process of revising initial responses heavily depends on a powerful proprietary LLM (gpt-3.5-turbo), which introduces potential operational costs and reliance on an external model's capabilities and inherent biases.

def _tokenize_fn(strings: Sequence[str], tokenizer: transformers.PreTrainedTokenizer) -> Dict:
    """Tokenize a list of strings."""
    ids_list = tokenizer(
        strings,
        max_length=tokenizer.model_max_length,
        truncation=True,
        return_attention_mask=False
    )['input_ids']

    input_ids = []
    input_ids_lens = []

    for ids in ids_list:
        input_ids.append(torch.tensor(ids))
        input_ids_lens.append(len(ids))

    return dict(
        input_ids=input_ids,
        input_ids_lens=input_ids_lens,
    )

def preprocess(
    sources: Sequence[str],
    targets: Sequence[str],
    raws: Sequence[str],
    tokenizer: transformers.PreTrainedTokenizer,
) -> Dict:
    """Preprocess the data by tokenizing."""
    examples = [s + ' ' + t for s, t in zip(sources, targets)]
    examples_tokenized, sources_tokenized = [_tokenize_fn(strings, tokenizer) for strings in (examples, sources)]
    input_ids = examples_tokenized["input_ids"]
    labels = copy.deepcopy(input_ids)
    for label, source_len in zip(labels, sources_tokenized["input_ids_lens"]):
        label[:source_len] = IGNORE_INDEX
        
    examples2 = [s + ' ' + t for s, t in zip(sources, raws)]
    examples_tokenized2, sources_tokenized2 = [_tokenize_fn(strings, tokenizer) for strings in (examples2, sources)]
    input_ids2 = examples_tokenized2["input_ids"]
    raw_labels = copy.deepcopy(input_ids2)
    for label, source_len in zip(raw_labels, sources_tokenized2["input_ids_lens"]):
        label[:source_len] = IGNORE_INDEX
    return dict(input_ids=input_ids, raw_input_ids=input_ids2, labels=labels, raw_labels=raw_labels)


class SupervisedDataset(Dataset):
    """Dataset for supervised fine-tuning."""

    def __init__(self, data_path: str, tokenizer: transformers.PreTrainedTokenizer, instruction_type: str):
        super(SupervisedDataset, self).__init__()
        logging.warning("Loading data...")
        list_data_dict = load_dataset(data_path)['train']

        logging.warning("Formatting inputs...")
        if instruction_type == 'no_inst':
            prompt_input, prompt_no_input = PROMPT_DICT["no_instruction_input"], PROMPT_DICT["no_instruction_no_input"]
        else:
            prompt_input, prompt_no_input = PROMPT_DICT["prompt_input"], PROMPT_DICT["prompt_no_input"]
        sources = [
            prompt_input.format_map(example) if example.get("input", "") != "" else prompt_no_input.format_map(example)
            for example in list_data_dict
        ]
        targets = [f"{example['revised_output']}{tokenizer.eos_token}" for example in list_data_dict]
        raw = [f"{example['original_output']}{tokenizer.eos_token}" for example in list_data_dict]

        logging.warning("Tokenizing inputs... This may take some time...")
        data_dict = preprocess(sources, targets, raw, tokenizer)

        self.input_ids = data_dict["input_ids"]
        self.raw_input_ids = data_dict["raw_input_ids"]
        self.labels = data_dict["labels"]
        self.raw_labels = data_dict["raw_labels"]

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self.input_ids)

    def __getitem__(self, i) -> Dict[str, torch.Tensor]:
        return dict(input_ids=self.input_ids[i], raw_input_ids=self.raw_input_ids[i], labels=[self.labels[i], self.raw_labels[i]])

def transform_tensor(source, target):
    operations = Levenshtein.editops(source.tolist(), target.tolist())
    return [i[2] for i in operations if i[0]!='delete'], [i[1] for i in operations if i[0]!='insert']

@dataclass
class DataCollatorForSupervisedDataset(object):
    """Collate examples for supervised fine-tuning."""

    tokenizer: transformers.PreTrainedTokenizer

    def __call__(self, instances: Sequence[Dict]) -> Dict[str, torch.Tensor]:
        input_ids = [instance["input_ids"] for instance in instances]
        raw_input_ids = [instance["raw_input_ids"] for instance in instances]
        labels = [instance["labels"][0] for instance in instances]
        raw_labels = [instance["labels"][1] for instance in instances]

        max_len = max(max(len(seq) for seq in input_ids), max(len(seq) for seq in raw_input_ids))
        input_ids = [F.pad(seq, (0, max_len - len(seq)), value=self.tokenizer.pad_token_id) for seq in input_ids]
        raw_input_ids = [F.pad(seq, (0, max_len - len(seq)), value=self.tokenizer.pad_token_id) for seq in raw_input_ids]
        input_ids = torch.stack(input_ids)
        raw_input_ids = torch.stack(raw_input_ids)

        max_len_labels = max(max(len(seq) for seq in labels), max(len(seq) for seq in raw_labels))
        my_mask = torch.zeros(input_ids.shape[0], max_len_labels)
        my_mask_neg = torch.zeros(input_ids.shape[0], max_len_labels)

        # Positive masks
        for idx, exmp in enumerate(labels):
            operations, op=transform_tensor(raw_labels[idx], labels[idx])
            for j in operations:
                my_mask[idx][j]=1
            for j, _ in enumerate(labels[idx]):
                if labels[idx][j]==self.tokenizer.eos_token_id:
                    my_mask[idx][j]=1
                    break

        # Negative masks
        for idx, exmp in enumerate(raw_labels):
            op, operations = transform_tensor(raw_labels[idx], labels[idx])
            for j in operations:
                my_mask_neg[idx][j] = -1
            for j, _ in enumerate(raw_labels[idx]):
                if raw_labels[idx][j]==self.tokenizer.eos_token_id:
                    my_mask_neg[idx][j]=1
                    break
        labels = [F.pad(seq, (0, max_len_labels - len(seq)), value=IGNORE_INDEX) for seq in labels]
        raw_labels = [F.pad(seq, (0, max_len_labels - len(seq)), value=IGNORE_INDEX) for seq in raw_labels]
        labels = torch.stack(labels)
        raw_labels = torch.stack(raw_labels)
  
        all_input_ids = torch.stack([torch.cat([input_ids[i], raw_input_ids[i]]) for i in range(len(input_ids))]).view(-1, input_ids.size(1))
        all_labels = torch.stack([torch.cat([labels[i], raw_labels[i]]) for i in range(len(labels))]).view(-1, labels.size(1))
        all_attention_mask = torch.stack([torch.cat([input_ids.ne(self.tokenizer.pad_token_id)[i], raw_input_ids.ne(self.tokenizer.pad_token_id)[i]]) for i in range(len(input_ids))]).view(-1, input_ids.size(1))
        all_my_mask = torch.stack([torch.cat([my_mask[i], my_mask_neg[i]]) for i in range(len(my_mask))]).view(-1, my_mask.size(1))

        return dict(
            input_ids=all_input_ids,
            labels=all_labels,
            attention_mask=all_attention_mask,
            my_mask=all_my_mask,
        )


def make_supervised_data_module(tokenizer: transformers.PreTrainedTokenizer, data_args) -> Dict:
    """Make dataset and collator for supervised fine-tuning."""
    train_dataset = SupervisedDataset(tokenizer=tokenizer, data_path=data_args.data_path, instruction_type=data_args.instruction_type)
    data_collator = DataCollatorForSupervisedDataset(tokenizer=tokenizer)
    return dict(train_dataset=train_dataset, eval_dataset=None, data_collator=data_collator)

def train():
    parser = transformers.HfArgumentParser((ModelArguments, DataArguments, TrainingArguments))
    model_args, data_args, training_args = parser.parse_args_into_dataclasses()

    model = transformers.AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
        model_args.model_name_or_path,
        cache_dir=training_args.cache_dir,
    )
    
    tokenizer = transformers.AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(
        model_args.model_name_or_path,
        cache_dir=training_args.cache_dir,
        model_max_length=training_args.model_max_length,
        padding_side="right",
        use_fast=False,
    )
    
    if tokenizer.pad_token is None:
        tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token
        tokenizer.pad_token_id = tokenizer.eos_token_id

    data_module = make_supervised_data_module(tokenizer=tokenizer, data_args=data_args)
    
    trainer = Trainer(model=model, tokenizer=tokenizer, args=training_args, **data_module)
    trainer.train()
    trainer.save_state()
    trainer.save_model(output_dir=training_args.output_dir)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    train()

Main Contributions:The paper addresses the challenge of selecting the most appropriate Large Language Model (LLM) for fine-tuning under resource constraints. It introduces the concept of the 'pre-power phase' in fine-tuning scaling curves, preceding the well-known 'power phase', which existing Scaling Laws fail to capture. The main contribution is the development of a 'Rectified Scaling Law' that incorporates a 'pre-learned data size' term to accurately model this phase transition. Based on this new law, the paper proposes a novel LLM selection algorithm called 'Accept then Stop' (AtS) which can select near-optimal models with hundreds of times less resource consumption than traditional methods, significantly outperforming existing baselines.
Methodology:The core methodology involves formulating LLM selection as a performance prediction task leveraging Scaling Law. A 'Rectified Scaling Law' is introduced, defined as ˆL(D) = B / (Dl + D^β) + E, where D is the training data size, Dl is the pre-learned data size, β is the learning difficulty, B adjusts initial test loss, and E is the optimal loss. This law is theoretically proven to capture the non-monotonic derivative corresponding to the pre-power and power phases. The 'Accept then Stop' (AtS) algorithm starts by fine-tuning a model on progressively smaller subsets (halving the data size). It fits a linear regression model to the log-log loss-size pairs and stops when a new point deviates significantly from this linear trend (indicating the pre-power phase transition). The full fine-tuning performance is then extrapolated using the fitted linear function of the 'power phase' data points. Parameters for scaling laws are fitted by minimizing Huber loss using the `scipy` optimization package.
Experimental Setup:Experiments were conducted on 30 diverse open-sourced LLMs, ranging from 100 million to 7 billion parameters, including encoder-decoder (e.g., T5, BART) and decoder-only (e.g., GPT2, Cerebras-GPT, Phi, OPT) architectures. Fine-tuning tasks included machine translation (WMT19 English-Chinese), paragraph summarization (Gigaword), and multi-task instruction tuning (FLAN). To study scaling behavior, subsets of datasets with sizes D ranging from 200 to 1,638,400 samples were used. Each fine-tuning experiment was run three times with different random seeds. Optimization used AdamW and a cosine learning rate scheduler, with hyper-parameter search for learning rates and batch sizes. Evaluation metrics for LLM selection were Pearson correlation coefficient (PearCorr) and Relative Accuracy (RelAcc). Baselines included ModelSize, ZeroShot, SubTuning, and two scaling-law-based fitting methods (OurFit and VanillaFit). Data budget ratio (γ) for selection varied from 1/512 to 1/8. All experiments were implemented using PyTorch and the HuggingFace library on NVIDIA A100 GPUs, consuming an estimated 20,000 GPU hours.
Limitations:The AtS algorithm experiences performance degradation when the data budget ratio (γ) is extremely small, resulting in all observed data points being within the pre-power phase. The paper also acknowledges a lack of a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanism of the pre-power phase and the phase transition itself. The current study does not extend the benefits of the proposed Scaling Laws to other fine-tuning strategies such as RLHF or LoRA, nor to other resource constraint types.
Future Research Directions:Future work could focus on developing new algorithms that can make accurate performance predictions even when observations are solely from the pre-power phase (i.e., with extremely small data budgets). Experiment:def log_ratio_modulated_loss(
    self,
    policy_chosen_logps: torch.FloatTensor,
    policy_rejected_logps: torch.FloatTensor,
    reference_chosen_logps: torch.FloatTensor,
    reference_rejected_logps: torch.FloatTensor,
) -> torch.FloatTensor:
    tau = 0.05
    pi_logratios = policy_chosen_logps - policy_rejected_logps
    ref_logratios = reference_chosen_logps - reference_rejected_logps
    logits = pi_logratios - ref_logratios
    logits = logits * self.beta
    # Modulate the mixing coefficient based on the log ratio magnitudes
    log_ratio_modulation = torch.sigmoid(logits / tau)
    logistic_component = -F.logsigmoid(logits)
    exp_component = torch.exp(-logits)
    # Blend between logistic and exponential component based on log ratio modulation
    losses = logistic_component * (1 - log_ratio_modulation) + exp_component * log_ratio_modulation
    return losses

Main Contributions:The paper addresses the limitation of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in language generation, which primarily uses the local logarithmic score (cross-entropy loss). It proposes a straightforward strategy to adapt non-local strictly proper scoring rules, such as the Brier score and Spherical score, for training language generation models. The key finding is that simply substituting the loss function with these alternative strictly proper scoring rules, especially during fine-tuning, can lead to substantial improvements in model generation capabilities, even scaling up to large language models (LLMs) like LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B. The work also introduces score smoothing and a masked logarithmic score to enhance its effect for arbitrary scoring rules.
Methodology:The core methodology involves adapting non-local strictly proper scoring rules to sequence prediction by distributing the scoring rule at the token level. This approach decomposes the sequence probability into a product of conditional token probabilities, allowing for optimization of well-calibrated conditional predictions at each time step. The paper utilizes two classic non-local strictly proper scoring rules: the Brier score and the Spherical score, and also explores their generalized forms (alpha-power score and pseudo-spherical score). To address issues with label smoothing for arbitrary scoring rules, a general technique called score smoothing is introduced. Furthermore, a masked logarithmic score is proposed to enhance the smoothing effect, particularly when other scoring rules might ignore the smoothing term due to flat loss landscapes.
Experimental Setup:Experiments were conducted on machine translation and abstractive summarization tasks. Machine translation benchmarks included WMT14 English-French (En-Fr), WMT14 English-German (En-De), and TED bilingual datasets (10 directions). Model architectures varied from Transformer-base and Transformer-big for smaller scale experiments to Large Language Models (LLMs) like LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B for larger scale evaluations. Limitations:When training language models from scratch, alternative strictly proper scoring rules (Brier, Spherical) exhibited slower convergence and inferior performance compared to the logarithmic score, which the authors hypothesize is due to distinct learning dynamics and hyperparameter settings optimized for the logarithmic score. The logarithmic score itself is criticized for being unbounded and sensitive to small perturbations. Score smoothing, when applied without enhancement, might be ignored by some scoring rules (e.g., Spherical score) because their corresponding loss functions are relatively flat around the optimal point. The equivalence between alpha-entmax loss and alpha-power loss does not hold when the probability of the ground truth word is zero, which means gradients cannot be transmitted for probability-based loss functions in such sparse transformation scenarios. The investigation into scoring rules as beam search objectives was not exhaustive.
